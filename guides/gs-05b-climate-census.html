3. Concept notes

🔥 Why the monsoon exists. Land heats faster than sea; summer low pressure over India (ITCZ shifts to 20–25°N, Tibetan low) pulls in moist SW winds from the Indian Ocean. The Westerly jet withdraws, then the monsoon bursts at Kerala in early June. In winter the pattern reverses: dry land-to-sea winds. The Arabian Sea branch gives 250–400 cm on the Western Ghats windward side, and the leeward side is a rain-shadow.
🔥 Retreating monsoon and the Coromandel. In Oct–Nov the low-pressure trough moves south. NE winds cross the Bay of Bengal, gather moisture and rain on the Tamil Nadu coast, S Andhra, SE Karnataka. The Coromandel is in the rain-shadow of the SW monsoon, so its rain is mostly from this season and from cyclones. ‘October heat’ (hot, humid, calm) comes before it.
🔥 Southern Oscillation / ENSO. The Southern Oscillation is the see-saw of air pressure between the east Pacific and the Indian Ocean/Australia. El Niño (warm central-east Pacific, weak trades) usually weakens the Indian monsoon; La Niña strengthens rainfall in SE Asia. Negative IOD (warm east, cool west Indian Ocean) = weaker monsoon; positive = stronger.
🔥 Why the south is hot all year. Closer to the Equator. Northern India is far from the sea (continental), so winters are cold. Western disturbances (Mediterranean) bring small winter rain to NW India, vital for Rabi crops.
⭐ Green Revolution. Started in the 1960s with HYV wheat and rice seeds. It needed fertiliser, pesticides and assured irrigation, so it began in Punjab, Haryana and western UP. After 1991 reforms, small farmers faced higher costs as subsidies fell. Irrigation + capillary action has made some Green Revolution soils saline.

4. Fact tables

5. Memory aids

  • Wind names: Loo = Lahore-to-Lucknow plains; Kalbaisakhi = Kolkata; Bardoli = Brahmaputra (Assam); Mango = Malabar side.
  • Six seasons (2 months each, from March): Vasanta (Mar–Apr) → Grishma (May–Jun) → Varsha (Jul–Aug) → Sharada (Sep–Oct) → Hemanta (Nov–Dec) → Shishira (Jan–Feb).
  • Confusable pairs: Mawsynram (highest average) vs Cherrapunji; Khasi Hills vs Garo/Jaintia; Bhangar (old) vs Khadar (new); Gondwana vs Tertiary coal; Malabar (Kerala) vs Konkan (Daman–Goa).
  • Rainfall ladder for forests: Evergreen >200 → Moist deciduous 100–200 → Dry deciduous 70–100 → Thorn <50 cm.
  • Odisha leads: chromite, manganese, bauxite, iron-ore belt. Rajasthan leads: gypsum, lead-zinc.

6. Trap alerts

  • Same wind, different state: Nor’wester = Kalbaisakhi (Bengal) = Bardoli Chheerha (Assam). Options will mix them with Loo and Mango showers.
  • Foreign winds (Bora, Mistral, Chinook) are used as distractors for Loo. Chinook is warm but American.
  • ‘Highest rainfall’ with Cherrapunji and Mawsynram both listed: Mawsynram. ‘Which hills’: Khasi.
  • Teak appears in the moist list in the source, but the shift key for ‘common to moist and dry’ was not extracted: [VERIFY].
  • Silver fir, deodar, oak belong to montane forests, not deciduous.
  • Coromandel rain = Bay of Bengal moisture, not the Arabian Sea and not ‘only cyclones’.

  • Southern Oscillation is not the same as El Niño or La Niña.
